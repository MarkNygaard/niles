---
title: "Why Niles"
description: "Who Niles is for, who should stay on Home Assistant, the ideas it is built on, and what leaves the house."
---

Niles replaces Home Assistant for one kind of household. It has a focused
set of devices, mostly Zigbee through Zigbee2MQTT and a few Wi-Fi ones. It
wants a spoken command answered in well under a second. It would rather name
its devices once than click through setup screens, and it wants a language
model built in. Somebody in the house runs a Kubernetes cluster.

Home Assistant solves a different problem, the widest device coverage for
people who don't run infrastructure, and that makes it heavy and led by its UI.
Niles makes the opposite trade. It supports fewer devices and assumes more
of the person who installs it.

## Who should stay on Home Assistant

- A house with lots of vendor Wi-Fi devices, Z-Wave or Matter.
- A family that needs a polished setup UI.
- Anyone who doesn't want to run a cluster, a Postgres database and an MQTT
  broker.

Niles is also young. Its releases are 0.x, and breaking changes are
expected.

## What Niles is not

- **A design or planning tool.** Voice is a poor medium for code, design or
  long plans. Niles files the task, a Linear issue for example, and tells you
  when something is ready. The thinking happens at a computer.
- **A voice OS.** Niles is the server. Satellites are dedicated hardware that
  talk to it.
- **An LLM training framework.** It uses models. It doesn't train them.

## How it stays fast

A spoken command never waits on something it doesn't need: a plugin, a
second model or a cloud call.

**The wake word is heard on the satellite.** A model trained for "Niles" runs
on the satellite's ESP32-S3, so nothing streams until the word is said. The
satellite then streams raw 16 kHz audio to Niles over TCP.

**Tiers by trigger.** Fixed phrasings for lights, blinds, scenes, music, the
TV, timers, heating, the weather and the clock are matched by regex and carried
out with no model at all. A fast model (Cerebras by default) takes the rest,
with only the tools that request is likely to need. The assistant, Claude Code
or ChatGPT on the household's own subscription, is slower and better at
writing. By voice it gets only the turns that need it, such as making a rule.
In the app's chat it answers whatever the fixed phrasings don't, and it is the
only one that reads attached files and writes documents.

**Tools, not search, for state.** The model asks the device registry for what
is on through tool calls, instead of searching a pile of text about the house.

**The answer comes from the satellite.** The room's Sonos is turned down while
Niles listens and speaks. It is something the model can play music on, never
where the answer comes from.

See [the voice loop](/docs/architecture/voice-loop) for each step.

## The house is described upstream

Rooms and devices are the names you give them in Zigbee2MQTT, as
`room/device`: `kitchen/ceiling`, `living_room/floor_lamp`. Niles keeps no
device database of its own, and no screen in the app creates a room. The app
changes values and presses switches. Name things well once and every new
device turns up in the right room. See
[Naming your devices](/docs/setting-up/naming).

## One house, several people

The house has one state: one set of devices, one lighting curve, one set of
scenes. Two people can't both own the curve.

People get personal references instead. "My calendar" and "wake me up" mean
the speaker's own calendar and alarm, found from the voice Niles recognised or
the person signed in to the app. A voice Niles doesn't know reaches nobody's
calendar, mail or notes. Voice prints stay in the house's own database, and
only people who enrolled are recognised.

## The model approves nothing

The model never decides an approval. When it wants to send a mail or use a
tool set to ask first, Niles holds the exact call and shows it to you. What
runs after your yes is what you saw. A mail is never sent without a press in
the app: never by voice, and no setting turns that off.

What a web page, a file, a tool result or an add-on says is information to
the model, never an instruction. A PDF that says "ignore your rules" is read,
not obeyed.

## What leaves the house

There is no telemetry. Niles sends nothing about you or your use of it to its
authors or anyone else. The services it does use, by default:

| Service | What goes there |
| --- | --- |
| Speech to text (ElevenLabs Scribe, or any OpenAI-compatible server) | The audio of each command after the wake word |
| The fast model (Cerebras, or any OpenAI-compatible server) | The words of a command no fixed phrasing caught |
| Claude or ChatGPT, if switched on | Chat messages, attached files, rules to work out, and the background review |
| The add-ons you connect | What each one needs to answer |
| GitHub | Signing in, and add-on downloads and update checks |

Speaking the replies (Piper), recognising voices and the fixed phrasings all
run on your own Niles. The full list is on
[Security and privacy](/docs/using/security#what-leaves-the-house).

## Extending it

Built-in integrations (Sonos, LG TVs, tado°, calendars, Strava and the rest)
are crates in the repository, held to its tests. A remote MCP tool server,
such as Linear's, brings a service's own tools. For the rest there are
[add-ons](/add-ons): WebAssembly components installed from a GitHub release.

An add-on reaches only what its manifest declares and the household approved.
It gets no undeclared hosts, no other add-on's secrets, no database, no
conversations and no code in the app. That limits what an add-on can reach. It
does not make one trustworthy, so the approval screen says plainly what is
granted, and an update that asks for more is approved again.
